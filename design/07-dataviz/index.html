The line that rises,
the datum that moved

Thirty-one years of spring-tide maxima at Harrow Mouth climb about 31 cm in total — and then, in 2011, the series falls by 28 cm in a single year. That fall is not the sea: it is the gauges being re-referenced from Ordnance Datum to Chart Datum. Everything in this story is drawn around that one fact, because a chart of a rising sea is only as honest as the floor it stands on.

02 One season, twelve gauges Twelve small charts, one axis each, a shared crosshair. A surge arrives at the mouth and takes five days to reach the top of the estuary — that travel time is the whole point of the grid, so the twelve are drawn at the same scale and read as one event.

03 What a month looks like A box hides more than it shows, so the raw daily points are drawn behind every box: the spread, the skew and the outliers are visible, and the summary is a drawing on top of the evidence rather than a replacement for it.

04 Hours above the line A stack is only honest when the parts add up to the whole. Hours in each height band add up, so the top edge is the total: hours of flooding, not a shape. Bands are labelled inside where there is room, and named again in the table.

05 Nine places, then and now Two columns, one line per place: the level of the 1-in-50-year event in 1994 and in 2024. Sorted by rise, the order is an argument. The numbers are printed at both ends, so nobody has to read a distance as a quantity.

06 Every day of 2024 A calendar, because the question asked at the quays is not “what is the annual mean” but “which days do I move the boats”. Days over the flood line are ringed, and the legend states the count in words so the ring is not the only encoding.

07 How to read this, and what it does not say

How to read it

  • The baseline is the datum, not zero. Water level is a difference, so a line chart may start off zero; the axis says which datum, every time.
  • Bars start at zero. In the counter-example below the same data is drawn with a bar baseline at its own minimum, and the rise becomes a wall.
  • The y-axis carries its unit in the axis label, not only in the caption, because crops and screenshots lose captions.
  • Colour is never the only carrier. The palette is Okabe–Ito (safe for the common forms of colour-vision deficiency), every series is labelled directly, and thresholds are ringed and counted.
  • Hover is a convenience. Every mark is reachable by keyboard, the spoken value and the painted value come from the same code, and every figure has its numbers.

What these charts do not say

  • The numbers are invented. They are generated from a seed to exercise a design; no gauge exists. A chart that cannot say that in the file, not in a footnote, should not be published.
  • The gauge gap is not interpolated. Salt Pits and Mudmere lost 41 of the 60 days to a storm damage notice in the middle of the season; the lines break at the hole, the readout prints a dash, and their listed peak is the highest recorded day, which is not the highest day.
  • One year is not a trend. Sections 02, 03, 04 and 06 are 2024. The only series long enough to argue from is section 01, and it is 31 years.
  • The 1-in-50 levels in section 05 are modelled from the same synthetic series; they are consistent with it, not independent of it.
  • No confidence interval is drawn on the trend, because a fitted straight line over a storm-spike series deserves the argument, not a band.

The same data, badly: four ways this page could have lied

Provenance. Every number on this page is generated by data.js from seed 4122 — a deterministic pseudo-random series with a flood event, a datum step and a gauge gap built in. The estuary, the authority, the gauges and the towns are fictional.

Method. Charts are drawn in SVG by charts.js: linear scales, generated ticks, one shared tooltip, one keyboard cursor, and a table generated from the same array as the marks.

No network data, no chart library, no build step. Print gives one figure per page.
